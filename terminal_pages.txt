3.3.5.5 Passenger Security Screening 
An increasing number of airports are seeking to innovate within their checkpoints, not only to improve security 
outcomes, but also to improve operational efficiency and the passenger experience. Careful planning and 
innovative design can, therefore, support airports to effectively deliver on their vision for the future. 
Vision: 
The passenger security screening processes should aim to be as contactless and minimally intrusive as 
possible, using latest technology to minimize divesting and long transaction times. Enabled by risk-based 
screening and improved checkpoint design, the end to end security screening process will be seamless, 
secure, and fully optimized. Passengers will pass through an “on the fly” biometric control point. Identity will 
be verified to permit access to the security screening area and passengers will be directed to the next 
available lane. Screening equipment will dynamically adjust so that passengers and carry-on baggage are 
screened according to their individual risk score for that day/trip. 
Passengers’ belongings will pass through an advanced carry-on baggage screening system to check for 
concealed prohibited items or explosives. Electronics, liquids and other items will remain inside 
baggage-Passenger will proceed at walking pace through a walk-through scanner (there will be no special 
stance to adopt). The scanner will provide an instant message to operators who will advise the passenger if 
they are clear to go or if further screening is required. 
Policy: 
The design of security checkpoints should facilitate the efficient management of a necessary control process, 
with the objective to expedite clearance of persons or goods and prevent unnecessary operational delays. 
Checkpoint design should take into consideration the evolving screening processes and procedures, 
depending on regulatory requirements, changing risk levels and, last but not least, overall evolution of 
passenger handling.

3.3.5.6.1 General Considerations 
Passport or border control services (generally referred to as “Emigration” and/or “Immigration”) are provided 
by governments in order to examine passengers and crew on arrival/departure at/from ports of entry in order 
to establish whether they are properly documented and that they have the right to enter or exit the respective 
territory. These government agencies will typically require a review and approval process for all design 
solutions. On departure, the authorities track when the passenger departs the territory, and verify that the 
passenger is not departing for reasons of law enforcement. Passengers and crew should present the required 
travel documentation on arrival at primary inspection points. Some countries may also inspect documentation 
on exit from the territory. 
Emigration staff located at departure points need to have access to dedicated databases to validate the 
identity of outbound travelers. Passengers who are improperly documented will be stopped by staff and may 
be taken off-line for further scrutiny. 
Where required, terminal planners should provide suitably sized departure emigration control desks/kiosks. In 
addition to the main outbound emigration processing channels, common administration offices should also be 
provided. 
Departing passenger interview rooms should be provided in a landside location and should be considered as 
secure rooms. The layout should be determined following consultation with the local emigration and 
immigration government body. 
Each government has its own domestic legislative provisions regarding outbound controls of passengers and 
their baggage. Many governments, in light of perceived threats to border integrity, require routine inspection of 
some or all baggage of departing passengers. Screening baggage for explosive material is also 
commonplace, and permanent but flexible infrastructure should allow airlines to carry out such inspection, 
where required. 
3.3.5.6.2 Latest Trends in Passenger Processing 
Recently there has been a shift toward international cooperation on emigration and immigration issues and 
many States are moving toward a multilateral approach to passenger processing. This approach has been 
facilitated by the increase in the use of machine-readable passports and the capture and verification of 
biometric data. 
Border control authorities are increasingly considering self-service solutions for passenger processing. 
Furthermore, biometric validation may become more widely used in the future.

3.3.5.7.1 General Considerations

3.3.5.7.5 Considerations for Sizing the Departure Lounge Area 
For the proper design and sizing of the departure lounge area, some important factors need to be considered. 
The most important element defining the size, configuration and seating capacity is the number of passengers 
present during the relevant peak periods, which directly relates to the passenger dwell times (i.e., the amount 
of time spent by departing passengers before proceeding to the gate). The longer the dwell time in the area, 
the bigger this space has to be. This dwell time depends on several factors, such as: 
¢ Call to gate—In collaboration with the airlines, some airports decide to provide the gate information at a 
later stage. Depending on the strategy selected, this could increase the dwell time for passengers in the 
departure lounge area and the space/seating requirements 
¢ Availability of Retail and Food & Beverage (F&B)-A significant number of passengers is not present in the 
departure lounge area, as they dwell within the different categories of commercial areas. The availability 
and attractiveness of this commercial offer will have an impact on the actual demand for space in the 
departure lounge area 
¢ Airline lounges-Premium passengers may choose to spend most of their time at the airport using airline 
lounges, extracting themselves from any sizing requirements about the common departure lounge 
¢ Transfer passengers-At airports with significant shares of transfer passengers, the dwell time in the 
departure lounge can be increased, resulting in additional demand for that area. This is especially 
relevant when long connection times are expected 
© Contingencies, disruptions and delays—Airport planners should consider the impact that disruptions and 
delays may have on space requirements, what operational solutions may be provided, and how to 
safeguard space to ensure continuity of operations for the passengers on flights that are not disrupted

3.3.8.6 Hold Baggage Screening 
It is a mandatory requirement that all bags be security screened prior to loading into the aircraft hold. The 
ICAO, in Annex 17 ‘Safeguarding International Civil Aviation Against Acts of Unlawful Interference’, sets out 
security standards and recommendations. The implementation of these measures is defined by national 
governments or other responsible bodies some of which publish detailed planning guidelines, such as the US 
Transportation Security Administration's Planning Guidelines and Design Standards for Checked Baggage 
Inspection Systems. 
Itis important that the relevant requirements for hold baggage screening be clearly defined and understood. 
There may be additional requirements imposed from time-to-time, based on the security assessments of 
national governments. In some cases, where a One-Stop Security (OSS) arrangement between two states is 
in place, the flow of transfer passengers and bags can be expedited by eliminating the unnecessary 
duplication of security controls at the transfer airport. These bags can be fed directly into the sortation system. 
3.3.8.6.1 Hold Baggage Screening Technology 
Two basic methods are used to detect explosives in hold baggage: EDS, which use X-ray technology to 
create images of the contents of bags, and ETD, which uses such techniques as lon Mobility Spectrometry to 
capture and analyze the microscopic particles and vapors naturally emitted by organic substances. 
Developments in EDS technology have led to successive generations of equipment described as Standards 1, 
2 and 3. Although a simplification, the differences between the standards are as follows: 
e Standard 1-EDS equipment provides a view of the contents of a bag from a single angle 
e Standard 2-provides views from multiple angles 
e Standard 3-the latest, uses computed tomography x-ray to provide high resolution 3-D views of the 
contents of the bag 
The latest Hold Baggage Screening (HBS) systems are more capable and able to reduce the rate of rejected 
bags, but they are also larger and heavier than previous generations. The space and infrastructure required 
for these new units needs to be planned carefully with sufficient space around the screening equipment set 
aside to support maintenance and repair. In addition, their installation often requires structural enabling works 
and an upgraded power supply.

3.3.8.10 Baggage Make-Up 
Departing bags are delivered via a system of carousels, chutes, or laterals to baggage make-up positions 
where they are loaded into baggage carts or unit load devices for delivery to the apron and loading onto 
aircraft. The number of make-up positions allocated per flight will depend on the expected volume of baggage, 
the flight-build time and the number of segregations into which bags must be sorted. This can vary from one 
or two positions for small aircraft to ten or more for larger aircraft with complex terminating and transfer 
products. 
The baggage make-up room requires parking positions for empty and filled containers, dollies and/or carts 
with sufficient height for easy maneuvering of vehicles. Properly sized multiple lanes are required to enable 
easy traffic movement and maintain a safe and efficient operation, including easy access to and from the 
apron. 
The overall efficiency of the baggage make-up area will be affected by how the facility is operated and 
utilized. Common use facilities that are shared by multiple airlines and ground handlers and used by flight are 
much more efficiently utilized than make-up areas that are dedicated to specific users. 
Baggage system interfaces with staff in the baggage hall should be ergonomically designed. Baggage off-load 
levels within the baggage halls should be designed to be ergonomically suited to the local workforce and 
should adopt best international working practices, such that the risk of off-loading injuries should be 
minimized.
