2.1.1.3 Design Hour Rate (DHR) 
The definition and calculation of the appropriate Design Hour Rate (DHR) is discussed in 2.2.10.1 Selection of 
the Design Rate Definition. It is also described how to forecast the current level to future planning scenarios. 
The calculations require access to a full year of historical traffic schedule. The airport data records (AODB, 
FIDS, Data Warehouse) is the natural preferred data source, alternatively aviation data services (OAG, SRS 
analyzer etc.) may be able to provide schedules, however, they do not provide full coverage of all operations 
and passengers, which will impact the selection of the Design Hour Level. 
For the calculation of the Design Hour Rate, the flight schedule data may easily be aggregated to an hourly 
level, possibly split in various segments (domestic/international, arrival/departure etc.) 
2.1.1.4 Design Day Flight Schedule (DDFS) 
The development of a future Design Day Flight Schedule (DDFS) is relevant in a number of planning studies 
depending on the planning horizon, data availability and required level of granularity (see 2.2.10.3 Design Day 
Flight Schedules). 
The development of a future DDFS builds on a thorough analysis and understanding of the current schedule 
and earlier schedule development, which in turn requires access to historical flight schedules for several years 
(5+ years is recommended). As was the case for the DHR calculations, the airport data records (AODB, FIDS, 
Data Warehouse) is the natural preferred data source, alternatively aviation data services (OAG, SRS 
analyzer etc.) may be able to provide schedules. 
2.1.1.5 Demand Calculation 
Within the ADRM Facility Sizing Process, “demand” is defined as the number of people that undertake a 
specific terminal process over a period of time. 3.3.4.9 Demand-Capacity Assessment provides two 
alternative methods for calculating the demand required for future facility sizing, building on either the future 
Design Hour Level or a future Design Day Flight Schedule. In both situations, the expected passenger 
presentation at the processing facilities must be considered. 
Several data sources may be used to construct presentation profiles (‘when do passengers for flight X show 
up at the back of the line for processor Y?”) which can later be applied to the DDFS to calculate the demand. 
Boarding card scans from existing automated access gates at the processor provide the best possible data 
source as the timestamp and flight number may be combined with additional schedule information from the 
airport flight records to produce detailed presentation profiles for various passenger and time segments 
(domestic/international, weekday/weekend, morning/afternoon) or even on a route-by-route level. Similarly,

2.2.9.4.3 VVIP Aircraft Activity 
It is important to remember that not all airports are the same when developing a general aviation forecast. A 
key segment that may affect some airports is often called VVIP, or “very, very important person” activity. 
WIPs are most commonly royalty or political figures. Airports with VVIP activity often have a private apron 
and/or an exclusive FBO associated with this traffic. Although VVIP service is not common at most airports, it 
is significant to keep this type of activity in mind when developing a general aviation forecast. 
2.2.9.5 Military/Government ATM Forecast 
Historical military/government aviation activity should be identified using airport statistics. Work with airport 
management and staff who are in contact with representatives of the military to have a better understanding 
of the expected future levels of military and government activity. Often, military movements are limited and/or 
secretive, and therefore hard to forecast. 
2.2.9.6 Total ATM Forecast 
The total movements forecast should be derived by adding all the ATM forecasts described above. An overall 
tabular format of total movements should be created displaying each category as well as total movements, 
including compound annual growth rates. The results from each segment of the movements forecast will be 
used for the peak period analysis described in detail in the next chapter. 
2.2.10 Peak Period Forecast 
Traffic demand at an airport is subject to seasonal, monthly, daily, and hourly variations. These variations 
result in peak periods when the greatest amount of demand is placed upon facilities required to accommodate 
passenger and aircraft movements. Peaking characteristics are critical in the assessment of existing and 
planned facilities to determine their ability to accommodate forecast increases in passenger and operational 
activity throughout the study period. The objective of developing peak period forecasts is to ensure planned 
facilities are neither underutilized nor overcrowded too often. Exhibit 2.2.10 illustrates a distribution of active 
hours at a specific airport ranked from busiest to quietest over a period of one year. Typically, the selected 
design hour will not be the absolute busiest hour, but rather some measure of a typical peak that covers most 
of the year while excluding the extreme busy periods. The outcome of a peak period selection will result on a 
design rate and not a specific period of time. Therefore, the use of design rate is used to represent that traffic 
level.

Passenger peak period forecasts will usually cover the design rates for total, arriving, and departing 
Passengers, as well as other elements of traffic mix (e.g., domestic, international, transfer). This is necessary 
because of the different facility requirements associated with each of these traffic elements. The different 
design rates should be analyzed separately in accordance with the methodology described in the following 
subsection for each specific traffic segment. Therefore, ranking of the hours of the year should be conducted 
for each of the traffic segments that will be required as part of the planning exercise. 
To determine the current representative design rate, it is necessary to evaluate historical design rate activity 
for each required segment. Historical data should be obtained covering the annual traffic and the traffic during 
the representative design rate. It is recommended that the dataset has several data points from multiple years 
and all the passenger segments driving facilities’ requirements. 
The departure design rate determines requirements for facilities such as check-in, security screening, and 
gate hold rooms. Arriving peaks drive functional elements such as immigration, baggage claim, and greeter 
requirements. The total peak may drive overall circulation, concessions, and restroom requirements. It is 
important to note that these peaks can be distinct, occurring at different times of the day as a function of 
airline scheduling practices, the role of the airport in the regional or national system, and geography. 
Depending on the airport and scope of the forecast, further disaggregation of peak hour activity may be 
required: 
e By multiple terminals 
¢ Per individual subsystems 
ATM peak period forecasts will focus on total, arrivals, departures, international, domestic and possibly other 
service types. In addition, planners may also require forecasts for maximum number of aircraft on the ground 
for stand and gate planning.

It is recommended to base a simulation on a flight schedule as the main traffic demand input. Often, a 
24-hour schedule reflecting a “Typical Busy Day” or “Design Day” is used. This is the most realistic approach 
to model airport processes with the interaction and interdependencies of flight-driven events taking place at 
the airport and to capture all the peaks in the different airport facilities. Such a schedule is also referred to 
as the DDFS. (see 2.2.10.3 Design Day Flight Schedules for more information). 
If a past situation is to be modelled, it is recommended that the Airport Operations Data Base (AODB) or 
tower log for the last traffic year be analyzed to identify typical busy days that are suitable to be used for the 
simulation and its objective. !f a past situation is to be re-investigated, allocation (i.e., check-in, gate, stands, 
reclaim belts) and staffing information for the day to be simulated must be obtained for that specific flight 
schedule as well. (see 2.2.10 Peak Period Forecast for the selection of a past reference flight schedule and a 
representative busy hour rate). 
If a future situation is to be modelled and no forecast flight schedule is available, one or more flight schedule 
scenarios can be created as a baseline for the analysis. For this, historic flight schedules can be analyzed 
regarding their peak structures and characteristics of arriving, departing and transfer peak hours, which might 
occur during different periods. An assumption model for the airlines serving the airport, their fleet mixes, load 
factors and possible additional movements during peak times can be set-up. Based on these assumptions, an 
example scenario flight schedule can be prepared, discussed and agreed upon by the airport stakeholders. 
Sometimes, only peak hour numbers are given as traffic demand input for a simulation. Therefore, it is 
tecommended to build a realistic demand structure from arriving and departing movements that can be used 
as artificial traffic scenarios in the simulation. For certain very specific questions, a simulation can be 
conducted based on synthetic flows (e.g., continuous inflow to a security check-point). 
Process times describe the time spent in different activities required to be performed by a mobile element or 
flow object and, consequently, how long they are transacting there expressed in seconds or minutes per 
passenger. Throughput rates are measured in number of passengers per time interval (e.g., number of 
passengers per hour per security lane) can depend on the processor times, which in turn depend on the user 
characteristics (e.g., checking a resident passport is usually faster than checking a non-resident passport). 
When measuring process times, it is helpful to understand how the relevant process is modelled in the 
simulation so that the data collected includes the independent variables that lead to the processing time 
measurement. For example: 
e Are idle times of counters while waiting for a passenger walking from the common queue to the counter, 
included in the specified process time or modelled explicitly?

For the purpose of sizing a gate hold room, the largest aircraft is the one that offers the biggest seating 
capacity. When multiple aircraft can be accommodated on a stand (MARS), the highest seating requirement 
may be generated by two smaller aircraft parked at the same time, rather than one large aircraft. The planner 
must verify which configuration requires more seating capacity. 
Consideration must also be given to the aircraft boarding process, where passengers are called forward to 
present their documentation for final checks against the airline DCS. Airlines may wish to differentiate by 
aircraft class for their premium passengers. Sufficient space must be provided for queuing and the processing 
equipment at the head of the gate hold room.
