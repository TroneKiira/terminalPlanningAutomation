3.3.4 Terminal Capacity and the Level of Service 
The concept of Level of Service (LoS), as applied to airport terminal design, was originally developed by 
Transport Canada in the mid to late 1970s as the prevailing definitions of “capacity” were considered 
inadequate. In 1981, the Airport Associations Coordinating Council (AACC)-the precursor to ACI and IATA 
jointly published a study on airport capacity that resulted in the first edition of the Guidelines for Airport 
Capacity/Demand Management, which contained a tabular presentation of LoS guidelines by airport 
processing area, based on the Transport Canada concept. This guidance was further updated in 1990 and 
1996, after which it was incorporated into IATA's ADRM and remained largely unchanged through the 
9th edition (published in 2004). 
Over this period, the concept of LoS was applied in various ways for the design of new facilities, the 
expansion and monitoring of existing facilities, and as a benchmark that determines whether the contractual 
obligations of airport owners, operators and/or third party service providers are being met. It was also 
highlighted that the importance of the previous airport LoS guidelines was perceived to be higher in 
correlation with other key quantitative (e.g., waiting times, process rates) and qualitative (e.g., perceived 
service quality, information flow, wayfinding, walking distances) characteristics. These alternative key 
performance indicators are used in the industry, but often not with the same consistency as LoS guidelines. 
Taking some of these considerations into account, the LoS references were reviewed and refined in a way 
that incorporated waiting time aspects in addition to the spatial requirements. In this way, it is possible to 
determine a balanced LoS that neither under provides nor over provides and responds to a realistic design 
horizon for facility planning. This updated LoS methodology was released in the ADRM 10th edition and is 
now widely used. 
Terminal design and LoS should reflect the operating environment as well as the volume of passengers and 
baggage to be handled. Managing terminal capacity and designing with LoS in mind are key requirements in 
the development of cost-effective airports. These factors have long-term financial and operational implications 
for passenger facilities. 
Planners and decision makers must keep in mind that passengers visit an airport for one primary reason: to 
catch a flight. Passengers’ expectations and needs should be at the very heart of the planning process. The 
mark of a successful airport is its natural and unobstructed passenger flow between objectives, easy 
navigation through the terminal, simplicity and cost-effectiveness. 
It is important to understand and quantify the LoS currently being provided in order to establish an appropriate 
new LoS requirement. Existing LoS must be taken into account to ensure that the “stretch” to improve LoS is 
not as difficult or extreme as to discourage those tasked with making and managing the service 
improvements.

Several factors influence airport terminal Capacity. For processing facilities, capacity is influenced by three 
main components: 
* The passenger demand 
¢ The processing time (i.e., the time it takes to perform a transaction or other process for a particular 
passenger at a particular resource) 
These factors will have different values depending on the type of airport, its size and configuration. Passenger 
characteristics also vary according to the region they come from, the purpose of their travel and their flight 
destination. For example, a business traveler may travel with fewer bags and may arrive at the airport later 
than a leisure traveler. These two factors (amount of baggage and arrival time) will affect the space 
requirements for departure processes. Applying general rules-of-thumb (capacity formulas) for assessing 
capacity is an accepted method to estimate requirements. These methods are also useful in the preliminary 
planning phase to establish a rough order of magnitude for various facilities. Detailed design should make use 
of simulation modeling techniques to refine the space requirements and to assist with detailed LoS analysis. 
Demand also varies by the year, season, month, week and even by the time of day. Defining demand for the 
purpose of planning/designing airport subsystems is a complex exercise. It not only involves estimating the 
number of passengers who will need to complete a given process, but it must also address interdependencies 
between subsystems and how they affect the flow of passengers across the airport terminal facilities as a 
whole. See 2.2.10 Peak Period Forecasts for more information. 
3.3.4.6 Level of Service Concept 
The concept of Level of Service (LoS) is an aggregated framework for the design and expansion of facilities 
as well as for the monitoring of existing facilities. For processing facilities, both queueing time and space 
requirements must be considered together to provide a balanced LoS. For holding and circulation facilities, 
queueing time is not applicable and can be replaced by other service considerations, such as seat ratios. The 
general objective is to allow for a realistic design horizon for passenger facilities without over or 
underproviding. 
LoS is used to help determine facility requirements for a given design demand. Plans developed for a forecast 
busy hour will provide facilities that operate according to an optimum LoS aligned to an agreed future 
throughput. Until the airport reaches that forecast level of activity, the facilities will function at a higher LoS, 
falling in the Over-Design. Furthermore, because demand varies over time, the LoS will also vary. Planners 
should, therefore, consider targeting an optimum LoS in the knowledge that, during the busiest/peak traffic 
periods, the optimum LoS may not be achieved. Balancing investment decisions with LoS is a complex 
management and policy decision.

however, that different regions, countries and markets require adjustments to the airport environment to better 
equate with their service needs. The revised LoS benchmarks now include a range of values for space and

Impact of COVID-19 on the Level of Service Guidelines 
See 1.8 COVID19 Green Pages for additional information 
From the very early stages of the pandemic, one of the key protective measures that have been implemented 
across almost every country is a requirement to maintain a physical distance that reduces the risk of 
transmission. Physical distancing is an effective measure to limit transmission of SARS-CoV-2 and should be 
part of a comprehensive package of measures to limit the spread of COVID-19. 
However, one of the key challenges of the pandemic has been the lack of consistent application of the health￾related measures globally. In particular, where requirements have been applied different recommendations in 
terms of physical distancing have been proposed ranging between 1 and 2 meters. 
In an attempt to propose a harmonized approach to physical distancing both the WHO and ICAO established 
a recommendation to keep a distance of at least 1m which, in combination with other health related 
regulations (use of face masks, vaccination certificates, optimizing passenger flows etc), should provide the 
means for a safe and healthy journey. 
While there are different interpretations on how to measure the physical distance and how to apply it to 
queuing and holding areas of terminal buildings, it appears to be clear that the objective is to avoid potential 
droplets/aerosols from the nose and mouth of one passenger teaching the passengers standing immediately 
next to them. Therefore, it is fair to make the interpretation that a direct linear separation between two 
Passengers should suffice in order to comply with a physical distancing requirement of 1 meter. 
With all of this in mind, the current Level of Service Guidelines are perfectly able to accommodate those 
minimum requirements without any changes. Only minor adjustments to the layouts of queueing or seating 
areas should be required to accommodate the minimum requirements set by the WHO and ICAO. 
It should also be noted that while we recognize that regulatory mandates vary across different countries and 
regions imposing more stringent separation requirements than the minimum, it is IATA's view that these 
measures are likely to be temporary and will be lifted once the vaccination rates are high and consistent 
globally. This is also consistent with what has been observed in those countries with high vaccination rates 
and low number of infections where physical distancing requirements have been lifted in other public 
buildings, modes of transport and interior spaces. 
In addition, the Level of Service guidelines also address queueing times and seating availability. Both of these 
areas have also been impacted by some of the health-related restrictions (see Impact of COVID-19 on 
Terminal Capacity). While it is recognized that during times with additional processes or extra processing 
times the queueing objective defined in the LOS framework may not be achievable, the temporary nature of 
those restrictions should not have an impact on queueing requirements defined in the Level of Service 
guidelines. Similarly, current seating design makes it impossible to maintain the separation distances, but it 
should also be seen as temporary measure. Advancements in self check in and security screening will 
increase queuing efficiency. Other technologies exist or are being developed to flatten peaks using virtual 
queuing.”
